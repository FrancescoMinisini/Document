\begin{abstract}
Reinforcement learning has been proposed as a unified route to analog quantum gate design, in which
accuracy, leakage suppression, runtime and robustness against stochastic control errors are optimized
jointly. We report an independent reimplementation of the Universal cost Function control Optimization
(UFO) framework of Niu \emph{et al.} [npj Quantum Inf.\ \textbf{5}, 33 (2019)] for the two-qubit gate family
$N(\alpha,\alpha,\gamma)=\exp[i(\alpha\sigma^x\sigma^x+\alpha\sigma^y\sigma^y+\gamma\sigma^z\sigma^z)]$ on a
three-level gmon model, and compare its trust-region policy optimization agent with a direct gradient
optimizer that uses the same Hamiltonian and the same objective, the same training noise, but with different simulation parameters. The framework
produces high-fidelity analog gates, but three of its central claims are not supported. Against per-step
white control noise, the open-loop robustness of a pulse is fixed to leading order by its nominal fidelity
and its duration, so training under such noise cannot make a pulse more robust. Matched pairs of agents
and the direct optimizer confirm this, and the optimizer gains robustness only when it is trained against
quasi-static noise. In our implementation the direct optimizer also beats the learned controllers on every
nominal metric. Finally, every member of the benchmark subfamily $N(\alpha,\alpha,\pi/2)$ is an exchange
rotation by $\alpha-\pi/2$, so the reported order-of-magnitude runtime gain sits around the identity;
against a target-specific two-CNOT synthesis the learned pulses are at most twice as fast. 
% All code and data are public.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A quantum algorithm is written as a sequence of logical unitaries, but a quantum processor executes
continuous-time dynamics generated by an experimentally accessible Hamiltonian. On pre-fault-tolerant
superconducting hardware the translation between the two is not a formality: coherence budgets,
calibration drift, waveform distortion and multilevel structure all limit which circuits can actually
be run~\cite{Krantz2019,Kjaergaard2020,Koch2022}. Quantum control is therefore not a fine-tuning layer
on top of compilation, but one of the binding constraints on usable computational depth.

Rather than decomposing a target two-qubit unitary
into a fixed universal gate alphabet and paying the runtime cost of every elementary operation, one
may attempt to realize it directly through a single shaped analog pulse that exploits the native
interactions of the device~\cite{Niu2019,Chen2014}. If the target is well
aligned with the hardware's own entangling generator, the analog implementation can be substantially
shorter than any compiled circuit. There are two difficulties. Superconducting modes are
weakly anharmonic, so fast or aggressively modulated pulses drive population out of the computational
subspace~\cite{Motzoi2009,Werninghaus2021}; and the pulse that reaches the device is never the pulse
that was designed, because amplitudes fluctuate and Hamiltonian parameters are known only
approximately.

Niu \emph{et al.}~\cite{Niu2019} proposed to attack these requirements simultaneously rather than in
sequence. Their Universal cost Function control Optimization (UFO) framework combines three
ingredients: an analytic leakage estimate derived from a time-dependent Schrieffer--Wolff
transformation (TSWT), a scalar objective that adds leakage, boundary and runtime penalties to gate
infidelity, and a continuous-action reinforcement-learning (RL) formulation trained with trust-region
policy optimization (TRPO)~\cite{Schulman2015} in an environment where control noise is injected during
training. The reported outcome was strong: up to one order of magnitude lower average gate infidelity
than an RL agent trained without noise, around two orders of magnitude lower than a stochastic
gradient-descent (SGD) baseline, a fidelity standard deviation two orders of magnitude smaller than
either, and a one-order-of-magnitude runtime reduction relative to optimal gate synthesis on the
subfamily $\Ntgt{\alpha}{\pi/2}$ for $\alpha\in[1.2,1.7]$. The authors left one question explicitly
open: whether the advantage of RL persists when gradients are cheap enough for gradient-based
optimization to apply.

These are consequential claims, and they have been influential in establishing RL as a credible
language for robust pulse design~\cite{Bukov2018,Sivak2022,Khalid2023}. They have not, to our
knowledge, been subjected to an independent reimplementation. That is the purpose of this work.

\paragraph*{What this paper does.}
We rebuild the framework from scratch (multilevel gmon simulator, second-order TSWT leakage
estimator, exact bandwidth filter, UFO objective, TRPO agent and stochastic environment) and use it
to ask four questions. (i) Does the framework reproduce under an independent implementation, and with
what quantitative fidelity? (ii) How does the policy-gradient controller compare with a direct pulse
optimizer that computes exact gradients through the same simulator, with the same objective and the
same training noise? (Question left open by Ref.~\cite{Niu2019}) (iii) Does stochastic training make
the deployed pulses more robust, and if not, why not? (iv) What does the runtime comparison with gate
synthesis actually measure, given the structure of the benchmark family? Along the way we test how
much the conclusions depend on the free parameters the original paper does not pin down, the
actor--critic architecture and the UFO weights.

\paragraph*{What we find.}
The framework works as a pulse generator, but its central claims are not supported by controlled tests
in our implementation. Items 1 and 3 below do not depend on the implementation; items 2 and 4 do.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{White noise leaves nothing to train for.} Against per-step white control noise, the open-loop
robustness of any pulse is fixed to leading order by its nominal fidelity and its duration,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:whitenoise}, at a rate that we compute from the Hamiltonian and verify on every pulse we
produced (Sec.~\ref{sec:whitelimit}). Accordingly, four matched pairs of TRPO agents that differ only in
the training noise deploy pulses of the same robustness, and a direct optimizer gains robustness only when
it is trained against quasi-static noise, by about a fifth (Secs.~\ref{sec:matched}--\ref{sec:memory}).
\item \emph{The direct optimizer is ahead.} On the representative target $\Ntgt{2.2}{\pi/2}$ a single-start
Adam optimizer reaches about half the cost of our TRPO agents, with a pulse of $70\ns$ against $116\ns$,
and is the most robust controller at every noise strength (Secs.~\ref{sec:single}
and~\ref{sec:selected}). This compares one gradient optimizer with our agents; better-trained agents
could narrow the gap.
\item \emph{The benchmark family consists of exchange rotations.} Every member of $\Ntgt{\alpha}{\pi/2}$
is an exchange rotation by $\alpha-\pi/2$: the target at $\alpha=\pi/2$ is the identity, the
order-of-magnitude runtime gain of Ref.~\cite{Niu2019} lies around it, and every non-local member needs
two CNOTs, not three (Sec.~\ref{sec:structure}). Against that synthesis the converged learned pulses are
$1.0$--$2.0\times$ faster.
\item \emph{The runtime advantage belongs to analog control, not to learning.} The direct optimizer alone
is $1.9$--$3.75\times$ faster than two-CNOT synthesis, and its pulse durations track the exchange angle,
close to the shortest time in which the bandwidth-limited coupling can deliver it
(Sec.~\ref{sec:physicalinterp}).
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:framework} summarizes the framework and Sec.~\ref{sec:methods} our implementation;
Secs.~\ref{sec:single}--\ref{sec:runtime} present the nominal, robustness and runtime results; and
Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion} compares them with the original claims. The complete parameter set and the
sweeps that fix the free parameters are in Appendices~\ref{app:config} and~\ref{app:modelsel}.

\section{Control framework}
\label{sec:framework}

This section summarizes the framework of Ref.~\cite{Niu2019}.

\subsection{Model and leakage bound}
\label{sec:model}
\label{sec:tswt}

The platform is the gmon architecture: two weakly anharmonic superconducting modes with local drive and
frequency control, coupled through a flux-tunable interaction~\cite{Chen2014}, so that the entangling
interaction is itself a control. In the rotating-wave approximation the two-mode Hamiltonian
is~\cite{Niu2019}
\begin{align}
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{RWA}}(t) =\;&
\frac{\eta}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\hat{n}_{j}\bigl(\hat{n}_{j}-1\bigr)
+ g(t)\bigl(\hat{a}_{2}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{1}+\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{2}\bigr)
\nonumber\\
&+\sum_{j=1}^{2}\delta_{j}(t)\hat{n}_{j}
\nonumber\\
&+\sum_{j=1}^{2} i f_{j}(t)\Bigl(\hat{a}_{j}e^{i\phi_{j}} - \hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}e^{-i\phi_{j}}\Bigr),
\label{eq:hrwa}
\end{align}
with $\hat{n}_j=\hat{a}_j^\dagger \hat{a}_j$, anharmonicity $\eta$, coupling $g$, detunings $\delta_j$, and
drives of amplitude $f_j$ and phase $\phi_j$: seven control channels. Projected onto the two lowest
levels of each mode it becomes
\begin{align}
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{qb}}(t)=\;&\frac{g(t)}{2}\bigl(\sigma_{1}^{x}\sigma_{2}^{x}+\sigma_{1}^{y}\sigma_{2}^{y}\bigr)
\nonumber\\
&+\sum_{j}\Bigl[\frac{\delta_{j}}{2}\sigma_{j}^{z}
- f_{j}\bigl(\sin\phi_{j}\,\sigma_{j}^{x}+\cos\phi_{j}\,\sigma_{j}^{y}\bigr)\Bigr],
\label{eq:hqb}
\end{align}
an $XY$-type entangler with arbitrary single-qubit rotations, which is enough for universal two-qubit
control. We use Eq.~\eqref{eq:hqb} for interpretation only: all dynamics are propagated with the
multilevel Eq.~\eqref{eq:hrwa}, so that leakage is kept.

Because $\eta$ is finite, the controls that produce the logical rotations also couple the computational
sector $\Omega_0=\mathrm{span}\{\ket{00},\ket{01},\ket{10},\ket{11}\}$ to the leakage sector
$\Omega_1=\mathrm{span}\{\ket{02},\ket{20},\ket{12},\ket{21}\}$. Decompose
\begin{equation}
\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}_0+\hat{H}_1(t)+\hat{H}_2(t),
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
into the diagonal drift $\hat H_0$, a block-diagonal part $\hat H_1$ and a block-off-diagonal part
$\hat H_2$, with $\Delta$ the minimum gap between $\Omega_0$ and the leakage sectors and
$\epsilon\sim\|\hat H_1\|\sim\|\hat H_2\|\ll\Delta$ the control scale. A time-dependent Schrieffer--Wolff
transformation (TSWT) moves to a frame $\ket{\tilde\psi}=e^{-\hat S(t)}\ket{\psi}$, with anti-Hermitian
$\hat S$, in which the Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbb{H}}(t)=-i\,\frac{d e^{-\hat{S}}}{dt}e^{\hat{S}} + e^{-\hat{S}}\hat{H}e^{\hat{S}},
\label{eq:tswt}
\end{equation}
and chooses $\hat S$ to cancel the block-off-diagonal terms order by order. At second order a residual
$\Hod=O(\epsilon^3/\Delta^2)$ is left, and it bounds the coherent leakage accumulated over a gate of
duration $T$ (Appendix~\ref{app:tswt}):
\begin{align}
\Ltot(T)\;\lesssim\;&
\frac{\|\Hod(0)\|}{\Delta(0)}+\frac{\|\Hod(T)\|}{\Delta(T)}
\nonumber\\
&+\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{\Delta(t)^{2}}\left\|\frac{d^{2}\Hod(t)}{dt^{2}}\right\|dt .
\label{eq:leakbound}
\end{align}
The two boundary terms penalize residual off-diagonal coupling at the pulse ends. The integral penalizes
rapid modulation: a pulse can be small in amplitude at every instant and still leak if it changes too
quickly. Because the bound is written in terms of the Hamiltonian and its derivatives, it can be
accumulated during propagation.

\subsection{The UFO objective}
\label{sec:ufo}

Let $U(T)=\mathcal{T}\exp[-i\int_0^T \hat H_{\mathrm{RWA}}(t)dt]$ and let $U_{\mathrm{proj}}$ be its
restriction to $\Omega_0$. The gate fidelity with respect to $\Utgt$ is
\begin{equation}
F=\frac{1}{16}\bigl|\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(U_{\mathrm{proj}}^{\dagger}(T)\,\Utgt\bigr)\bigr|^{2},
\label{eq:fidelity}
\end{equation}
which equals $1$ when the implemented gate matches the target up to a global phase. The objective adds
three penalties~\cite{Niu2019},
\begin{align}
C(\chi,\beta,\mu,\kappa)=\;&
\chi\bigl[1-F\bigr]+\beta \Ltot+\kappa T
\nonumber\\
&+\mu\!\!\sum_{t\in\{0,T\}}\!\!\bigl[g(t)^2+f_1(t)^2+f_2(t)^2\bigr].
\label{eq:ufo}
\end{align}
for leakage, for runtime and for control envelopes that do not vanish at the pulse edges. All four terms
are soft constraints: every control channel stays available, and the optimizer trades the objectives
against one another.

\subsection{Reinforcement-learning formulation}
\label{sec:rl}

The pulse is built step by step. Time is discretized in steps $\Delta t$; the state presented to the
agent is the propagator accumulated so far, $s_n\sim U_n$; the action is the control vector for the next
interval; and the environment propagates
\begin{equation}
U_{n+1}=\exp\!\bigl[-i\Delta t(\hat{H}_{n+1}+\delta\hat{H}_{n+1})\bigr]U_n ,
\label{eq:envstep}
\end{equation}
where $\delta\hat H$ is the stochastic perturbation used in noisy training. The reward is the negative
running UFO cost, $r_n=-c_n$. An episode ends at $T_{\max}$ or at the first time the cost falls below a
threshold, so the agent optimizes the stopping time together with the pulse shape, unlike a
fixed-horizon pulse optimizer. The policy is trained with TRPO~\cite{Schulman2015}, which maximizes the
importance-weighted surrogate
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\bm\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}\!\left[
\frac{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(a|s)}{\pi_{\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{old}}}(a|s)}\hat{A}(s,a)\right]
\;\;\text{s.t.}\;\;
\mathbb{E}_s\bigl[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigr]\le\delta_{\mathrm{KL}} .
\label{eq:trpo}
\end{equation}
where the constraint limits how far one update can move the policy.

In noise-optimized training, Gaussian fluctuations are added to the control amplitudes at every step, so
that the objective becomes an expectation over both the policy and the noise,
$J(\bm\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{\tau,\xi}[C]$, and pulse trajectories that are fragile under small
perturbations earn a poor average return. We call \emph{nominal} an agent trained in the deterministic
environment and \emph{noise-optimized} one trained in the stochastic environment. They differ only in the
training distribution.

Two details of this construction matter later. First, the agent observes the propagator produced by
the \emph{noisy} dynamics, so during training it can react to the realized noise; the pulse that is
deployed and evaluated is the open-loop plan of its mean action in the noise-free environment, which
cannot. Second, every term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ufo} is evaluated on the noisy trajectory, including the
leakage bound, whose time derivatives respond to the noise itself (Sec.~\ref{sec:noisyleak}).

\subsection{Target family and synthesis reference}
\label{sec:targets}

Benchmarking uses the family
\begin{equation}
\Ntgt{\alpha}{\gamma}=\exp\!\bigl[i\bigl(\alpha\sigma_1^x\sigma_2^x+\alpha\sigma_1^y\sigma_2^y+\gamma\sigma_1^z\sigma_2^z\bigr)\bigr].
\label{eq:family}
\end{equation}
The equal $XX$ and $YY$ coefficients mirror the exchange structure of the gmon coupling. Up to
single-qubit rotations the family contains SWAP, iSWAP, CNOT and CZ, as well as the fermionic SWAP and
Givens rotations of Jordan--Wigner-mapped fermionic simulation~\cite{Niu2019}.

The runtime reference of Ref.~\cite{Niu2019} is a generic circuit decomposition: three CNOTs and four
layers of single-qubit rotations, the Vatan--Williams construction, which is optimal for an
\emph{arbitrary} two-qubit unitary~\cite{VatanWilliams2004}. With the gate times assumed there,
$T_{1q}=20\ns$ and $T_{\mathrm{CNOT}}=45\ns$, it takes
\begin{equation}
\Tsyn\approx 215\ns .
\label{eq:tsyn}
\end{equation}
We keep this number for comparability, but it is not the optimal synthesis of each member of the family:
for the subfamily $\gamma=\pi/2$ studied here two CNOTs suffice, which take $150\ns$
[Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsyntgt}], and three members need no entangling gate at all (Sec.~\ref{sec:structure}).

\section{Numerical implementation}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Simulator}

Each mode is truncated to three levels, giving $d_{\mathrm{phys}}=9$ and $d_{\mathrm{comp}}=4$, and
one-step propagation uses exact diagonalization of the Hermitian generator. The anharmonicity is
$\eta=-200\MHz$, which also sets the gap scale entering Eq.~\eqref{eq:leakbound}. Control amplitudes are
bounded at $20\MHz$, well below $|\eta|$ so that the perturbative regime $\epsilon/\Delta\ll1$ is
respected, and phases are mapped to $[0,2\pi]$.

Before reaching the Hamiltonian, the piecewise-constant controls pass through the two-pole
double-exponential filter of Ref.~\cite{Niu2019}, implemented exactly: with
$a=\exp(-\pi B/f_s)$, $B=10\MHz$ the bandwidth and $f_s=1/\Delta t$ the sampling rate,
\begin{equation}
\tilde u_k=(1-a)^2 u_k + 2a\,\tilde u_{k-1} - a^2\,\tilde u_{k-2}.
\label{eq:filter}
\end{equation}
The filter keeps the optimizer from exploiting control transitions sharper than a waveform generator can
produce, and it interacts directly with the non-adiabatic term of the leakage bound.

The leakage bound is evaluated numerically at every step. The estimator solves the second-order TSWT for
the generators $S_1$ and $S_2$, takes their time derivatives by finite differences, forms the residual
$H_{\mathrm{od}}^{(3)}$ and accumulates the boundary and integral terms of Eq.~\eqref{eq:leakbound}
incrementally, at a cost per step that does not grow with the pulse length (Appendix~\ref{app:algo}).

\subsection{Noise model}
\label{sec:noise}

At each step the realized control is $\bm{u}^{(\mathrm{noisy})}(t_k)=\bm{u}(t_k)+\bm{\xi}(t_k)$ with
$\bm\xi$ drawn independently from a zero-mean Gaussian of standard deviation $\snoise$. Perturbations
are applied to the coupling $g$, the detunings $\delta_{1,2}$ and the drive amplitudes $f_{1,2}$, and
additionally to the anharmonicity $\eta$; phases are left noise-free. Including $\eta$ is a
deliberate feature of the model: it probes sensitivity not only to imperfect pulse delivery but also
to uncertainty in a device parameter that the controller cannot actuate at all. The noise is added
after the bandwidth filter and is independent from one $2\ns$ step to the next, so it is white up to
the $250\MHz$ Nyquist frequency of the grid, above the $200\MHz$ gap to the leakage sector.
Ref.~\cite{Niu2019} discretizes its pulses more finely, in ``around one thousand time steps''; the
filter time constant of $32\ns$ is well resolved at $2\ns$, so the noise-free control problem is
essentially the same, but the step changes the meaning of $\snoise$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:noisescales}).

Training noise and evaluation noise are kept separate. The nominal TRPO agent is optimized without
perturbations. The noise-optimized TRPO agent and the Adam baseline are both optimized under this
perturbation model at $\strain=1\MHz$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:controllers}). All controllers are then probed
\emph{a posteriori} on the evaluation grid of Sec.~\ref{sec:robust}.

\subsection{Controllers compared}
\label{sec:controllers}

\paragraph*{Direct gradient baseline (Adam).}
For a fixed target and a fixed horizon $T$, the full discretized trajectory
$\{g_\ell,\delta_{j,\ell},f_{j,\ell},\phi_{j,\ell}\}_{\ell=0}^{N_T-1}$ is optimized directly with
Adam~\cite{Kingma2015} on the same multilevel model, the same filter and the same objective
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ufo}, differentiating exactly through the simulation: a GRAPE-style direct pulse
search~\cite{Khaneja2005,Koch2022} rather than a literal reproduction of the canonical algorithm. It is
trained under the same noise model as the noise-optimized agent: at every Adam step the loss is the
cost of one trajectory perturbed at $\strain=1\MHz$, a single-sample stochastic gradient of
$\mathbb{E}_\xi[C]$. Since the horizon is not a free variable of a single run, the procedure is
repeated over $T\in\{40,50,\dots,180\}\ns$, with $400$ Adam iterations per horizon, learning rate
$0.03$ and a single deterministic initialization (all amplitudes zero, phases $\pi$): $3.3\times10^5$
simulator steps in all, against $9.5\times10^7$ for each TRPO agent, a ratio of about $290$. Within a horizon
the stored plan is to iterate with the lowest sampled loss, and the optimizer's own choice of horizon
is the one with the lowest such loss. Because single-sample losses are noisy, we also re-evaluate every
horizon's plan in the deterministic simulator and report both selections. One horizon takes about
$100\,$s on one CPU core.

By the standards of Ref.~\cite{Niu2019} this baseline is modest. The SGD baseline there also used Adam
and a noisy objective, but averaged ten noisy trajectories per gradient step and received about $2000$
random restarts per target. What differs here is that the gradients are exact and are computed through
the same filtered multilevel simulator in which TRPO is trained. The comparison therefore addresses
the question Ref.~\cite{Niu2019} left open: whether the advantage of reinforcement learning persists
when gradient estimation is cheap enough for gradient-based optimization to apply, rather than
whether a weak baseline can be beaten. The baseline is a family of fixed-horizon problems, whereas TRPO
learns a variable-time policy; this structural difference is part of what the comparison tests.

\paragraph*{Nominal and noise-optimized TRPO.}
Both use the same actor--critic pair. The observation vectorizes the current $9\times9$ propagator into
real and imaginary parts and appends the normalized time index,
\begin{equation}
s_t=\bigl(\Re\,\mathrm{vec}(U_t),\;\Im\,\mathrm{vec}(U_t),\;t/T_{\max}\bigr),
\qquad d_{\mathrm{obs}}=163 .
\label{eq:obs}
\end{equation}
Actor and critic are multilayer perceptrons with hidden sizes $(64,32,32)$ and $\tanh$ activations, as
in Ref.~\cite{Niu2019}. The actor emits a mean $\mu_\theta(s_t)\in\mathbb{R}^7$ with a
state-independent trainable $\log\sigma$; raw actions $\tilde a_t=\mu_\theta+\sigma\odot\zeta_t$, with
$\zeta_t$ standard normal, are squashed by $\tanh$ into $[-1,1]^7$ and rescaled to physical bounds. The
critic outputs a scalar baseline for advantage estimation. At every iteration the mean-action rollout in
the noise-free environment is stored as a control plan; the controller reported for an agent is its
plan with the lowest nominal cost over the whole training run.

\paragraph*{Gate synthesis.}
The generic compiled reference of Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsyn} and the target-specific one of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsyntgt}, used for runtime only.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

Nominal quality is reported through the gate fidelity Eq.~\eqref{eq:fidelity}, the TSWT leakage
estimate $\Ltot$ with its boundary/integral split, the boundary penalty, the effective runtime and the
total cost Eq.~\eqref{eq:ufo}.

Robustness is characterized by two distinct quantities. The first is the average fidelity of the
\emph{noise-averaged channel} $\mathcal{E}$, estimated from the Monte Carlo ensemble of noisy projected
unitaries $\{K_j\}$ through the standard formula~\cite{Nielsen2002}
\begin{equation}
\Fbar=\frac{\sum_{j}\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(\Utgt U_j^{\dagger}\Utgt^{\dagger}\,\mathcal{E}(U_j)\bigr)+d^{2}}{d^{2}(d+1)},
\qquad d=4,
\label{eq:favg}
\end{equation}
with $\{U_j\}$ a two-qubit Pauli basis. The second is the variance across realizations of the
per-shot gate fidelity,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Var}(F)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\bigl[(F_\xi-\mathbb{E}_\xi[F_\xi])^{2}\bigr].
\label{eq:fvar}
\end{equation}
A controller with good mean fidelity but large variance is experimentally fragile, so neither quantity
alone settles the question.

\subsection{Parameters}
\label{sec:params}
\label{sec:modelsel}

All runs share the physical model above, a time step $\Delta t=2\ns$ and a maximum gate duration
$T_{\max}=180\ns$; the runtime in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ufo} is measured in units of $T_{\mathrm{norm}}=60\ns$.
Table~\ref{tab:config} in Appendix~\ref{app:config} gives the complete configuration of every experiment.

Two ingredients of the framework are not determined by the physics: the capacity of the actor--critic
networks and the relative weights in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ufo}. Both could silently drive the comparisons that
follow, so we fixed them first, with two sweeps on the representative target $\Ntgt{2.2}{\pi/2}$ under a
reduced budget (Appendix~\ref{app:modelsel}). Neither sweep gives a reason to depart from the published
choices: the layout $(64,32,32)$ and the weights
\begin{equation}
(\chi,\beta,\mu,\kappa)=(10,10,0.2,0.1)
\label{eq:weights}
\end{equation}
are within the seed-to-seed spread of the best configurations we found, and we use them throughout. The
sweeps also give that spread: three seeds of the same configuration reach $\Fbar=0.959$, $0.962$ and
$0.965$ at $\snoise=1\MHz$. Differences of a few $10^{-3}$ in $\Fbar$ between two individual runs are
therefore not significant on their own, which sets the resolution of every single-seed comparison below.

\section{Single-target benchmark}
\label{sec:single}

All controllers are first compared on
\begin{equation}
\Utgt=\Ntgt{2.2}{\pi/2},
\label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
a target that is neither near-identity nor a degenerate special case, that sits in the family used for
the original robustness analysis, and that isolates optimizer differences by holding target difficulty
fixed.

\subsection{Direct gradient baseline}
\label{sec:adam}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{adam_single_target_horizon_sweep.pdf}
\caption{Adam baseline on $\Ntgt{2.2}{\pi/2}$ versus gate horizon. (a) Nominal total cost, with the
optimum at $70\ns$ and the plan selected by the noisy objective at $60\ns$ both marked. (b) Boundary
and runtime penalties. (c) Nominal fidelity and total leakage. The optimum arises from the
simultaneous collapse of the boundary term and the rise in fidelity; longer horizons saturate in
fidelity and are penalized by runtime.}
\label{fig:adamsweep}
\end{figure}

The Adam baseline is trained under noise, but its plans are compared here in the deterministic
simulator. Under that re-evaluation the best horizon is $T^\star=70\ns$, with nominal cost $0.158$,
fidelity $0.9966$ and leakage $2.8\times10^{-4}$ (Table~\ref{tab:single}). The optimizer's own noisy
objective selects $60\ns$ instead, a plan with fidelity $0.982$; Sec.~\ref{sec:noisyleak} explains why it
prefers the shorter horizon. Optimized without noise at $70\ns$ the baseline does better still, with
fidelity $0.99939$ and cost $0.126$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:memory}); what then remains is the conditional phase
of the higher levels (Sec.~\ref{sec:hardware}).

The horizon dependence (Fig.~\ref{fig:adamsweep}) shows what sets the optimum. Below $60\ns$ the pulses
fail, with fidelity $0.73$ at $40\ns$ and $0.88$ at $50\ns$, although their leakage stays in the
$10^{-4}$ range: under strong time compression a pulse cannot be both accurate and zero at its ends, and
it is the boundary term, not the leakage, that grows. Between $60$ and $70\ns$ that term collapses and the
cost falls from $0.381$ to $0.158$. Longer pulses are slightly more accurate and less leaky (fidelity
$0.9971$ at $150\ns$) but pay for it in runtime, so the optimum is the best-balanced pulse and not the
most accurate one.

The $70\ns$ solution is $3.1$ times faster than the generic compiled reference of Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsyn}
and $2.1$ times faster than the two-CNOT synthesis of Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsyntgt}, before any policy learning
is introduced.

Trained in the deterministic environment with the same model and weights, the best TRPO policy reaches

\section{Robustness under stochastic control noise}
\label{sec:robust}

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}

Each stored control plan is replayed open loop under the noise model of Sec.~\ref{sec:noise} on a dense
grid of $3401$ noise strengths, $\snoise\in[0.1,3.5]\MHz$ in steps of $0.001\MHz$, with $60$ independent
realizations at each point. Sixty samples give a noisy point estimate, so the curves are read through an
exponentially weighted moving average along the $\snoise$ axis (span $50$ for the mean, $80$ for the
variance). The underlying scatter is shown in the figures and kept in the released data, and no other
post-processing is applied. Every controller is scored against $\Ntgt{2.2}{\pi/2}$, with the same seed at
each grid point.

Four controllers are compared: the two Adam plans of Table~\ref{tab:single}, the nominal TRPO plan and
the noise-optimized TRPO plan. The other noise-optimized candidate, the $104\ns$ plan of iteration $91$,
was evaluated too and lies between the two TRPO curves. Four individual plans cannot isolate the effect of
stochastic training, since each is one draw from a training process. We therefore first state what the
noise model allows any pulse to gain (Sec.~\ref{sec:whitelimit}), and then test it on the selected plans
(Sec.~\ref{sec:selected}), on the whole training histories of matched agents (Sec.~\ref{sec:matched}) and
with the direct optimizer (Sec.~\ref{sec:memory}).

\subsection{What white noise allows}
\label{sec:whitelimit}

Under this noise model the pulse shape cannot buy robustness. This is the white-noise limit of the
filter-function description of open-loop control~\cite{Green2012,Green2013,Cerfontaine2021}: the response
of a pulse to noise is the overlap of the noise spectrum with a filter function fixed by the pulse, and a
flat spectrum sees only the integral of that function, which the pulse shape does not change
(Appendix~\ref{app:firstorder}).

In the present model the argument is short. The noise is independent from one $2\ns$ step to the next.
Moving a kick to the end of the pulse conjugates its generator $G$ by the evolution so far, and as long as
that evolution keeps the computational subspace invariant, the first-order infidelity of the kick depends
only on the variance $V(G)=\mathrm{Tr}(PG^2P)/d-|\mathrm{Tr}(PGP)/d|^2$ of $G$ over that subspace $P$,
which conjugation leaves unchanged. To leading order the noise-induced infidelity is therefore
independent of the pulse shape and proportional to the duration,
\begin{align}
1-\Fbar(\snoise)&\simeq 1-\Fbar(0)+K\snoise^2T,
\nonumber\\
K&=\frac{d}{d+1}\bigl(2\pi\cdot10^{-3}\bigr)^2\Delta t\sum_c V_c ,
\label{eq:whitenoise}
\end{align}
with $\snoise$ in MHz, $\Delta t$ and $T$ in ns, and the sum running over the noisy channels. The
components of $G$ that lead out of the subspace, to states detuned by the anharmonicity, are partly
averaged during the step; we weight them by the zero-order-hold factor $\mathrm{sinc}^2(\omega\Delta t/2)$
at their transition frequency $\omega$. For the gmon model this gives $V=1/4$ for each detuning, $1.57$
for each drive, $1.07$ for the coupling and $0$ for the anharmonicity, and hence
$K=2.98\times10^{-4}\ns^{-1}\MHz^{-2}$. Two thirds of it come from the two drive amplitudes, and replaying
the pulses with the noise on one channel at a time reproduces each channel's share
(Table~\ref{tab:channels}).

The prediction holds on every pulse we produced. For the $160$ plans stored along the training of the two
TRPO agents (Sec.~\ref{sec:matched}), the measured excess infidelity per unit duration at $1\MHz$ is
$2.97\times10^{-4}$ and $2.99\times10^{-4}$ for the nominal and noise-optimized histories, with
interquartile ranges within $\pm2\%$. Evaluated with the exact response of every step instead of the sinc
approximation (Appendix~\ref{app:firstorder}), the first-order rate of those plans varies by four parts in
$10^4$: that is all the pulse shape can move. Excess infidelity and duration are correlated at $0.99$
across both histories, and the two Adam plans fall on the same line [Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism}(c)]. Away
from $1\MHz$ the measured rate drifts, to $3.4\times10^{-4}$ at $0.5\MHz$ and $2.7\times10^{-4}$ at
$2\MHz$, which reflects higher orders and the approximate treatment of the leaking components.

Against white control noise an open-loop pulse is robust only by being accurate and short, and a
noise-aware objective can improve robustness only through those two numbers. The rest of this section
tests that statement.

\subsection{Matched training histories}
\label{sec:matched}

The two TRPO agents were trained from the same seed, with the same architecture, weights, batch size and
stopping cost, for $100$ iterations (Table~\ref{tab:config}); the only difference was the training noise.
At every iteration each agent stored the deterministic plan of its current policy. Comparing those plans
iteration by iteration isolates stochastic training much better than comparing two selected plans, and
the spread along the history stands in, partly, for the spread across seeds.

We evaluated the $160$ plans of iterations $21$--$100$ at $11$ noise strengths between $0.1$ and
$3.5\MHz$, with $200$ realizations each. The result, Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism}(a), is the opposite of the
intended effect. At every noise strength the noise-optimized plan is \emph{less} robust than the nominal
plan of the same iteration in most iterations: it has the higher average fidelity in only $19$--$25\%$ of
them. The mean paired difference is $-1.7\times10^{-3}$ at $0.1\MHz$, $-3.5\times10^{-3}$ at $1\MHz$ and
$-1.5\times10^{-2}$ at $3.5\MHz$, and its $95\%$ block-bootstrap interval excludes zero up to
$2.5\MHz$. The plans of the two histories have similar durations (median $98$ and $103\ns$) and similar
nominal fidelity (median $0.9855$ and $0.9838$).

Two caveats bound this result. The difference we measure is of the size of the seed-to-seed spread found
in Appendix~\ref{sec:arch}, so a different pair of seeds could move it by a few $10^{-3}$ in either
direction; and the favourable comparison of the two selected plans above shows how easily one pair of
plans can suggest the opposite. What the data do rule out is an improvement of the size reported in
Ref.~\cite{Niu2019}: an order of magnitude in infidelity at $1\MHz$ would mean $\Fbar\approx0.996$ instead
of $0.96$, far outside the spread of either history.

\emph{Three seeds, with the leakage bound taken from the noise-free controls.} The noise-optimized agent
above was trained with a leakage bound that responds to the noise itself (Sec.~\ref{sec:noisyleak}), and
it is one seed. We therefore trained three further pairs of agents, with seeds $1$, $2$ and $3$ and the
settings of the matched iterations ($100$ iterations of $10\,000$ episodes, $C_{\mathrm{term}}=0.40$). In
these the noise-trained agent accumulates the leakage bound from the noise-free controls, so that the
training noise enters its objective through the fidelity alone. The runs use a batched implementation of
the environment, which agrees with the original one to $10^{-13}$ in the cost at every step but draws
different random numbers, so its seed $1$ is not the pair above. The $600$ plans were evaluated exactly
as before (Table~\ref{tab:seeds}).

whatever their seeds, gives differences between $-3.5$ and $+0.2\times10^{-3}$ at $1\MHz$, so what three

\subsection{What stochastic training changes instead}
\label{sec:noisyleak}

Stochastic training does not change the robustness of the deployed pulse, but in our implementation it
changes two other things: the objective that the agent sees, and the way the policy reacts to noisy
states.

\emph{The training signal is dominated by the noise itself.} Every term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ufo} is
evaluated on the noisy trajectory, including the leakage bound, whose second time derivative responds to
the step-to-step jumps of white noise. At $\strain=1\MHz$ the bound grows by $3.8\times10^{-3}$ per ns for
every plan we replayed, about $27$ times the leakage population actually present at the end of the pulse;
for the $116\ns$ nominal plan the term $\beta\Ltot$ is $4.5$, against $0.50$ for $\chi(1-F)$. In the noisy
objective, leakage thus acts as an implicit runtime penalty about $23$ times stronger than the explicit
one, $\kappa/T_{\mathrm{norm}}$, and one that is nearly blind to pulse shape. This explains two
observations. The Adam horizon search, whose sampled losses rise by about $0.04$ per ns from $60\ns$ on,
prefers the $60\ns$ plan to the more accurate $70\ns$ one. And the cost of a noisy TRPO rollout never falls
below the stopping threshold: all $1.05\times10^6$ training episodes of the noise-optimized agent ran to
the $180\ns$ ceiling, so the co-optimization of stopping time that distinguishes the RL formulation was
inactive throughout noisy training.

The bound is not the only reason. In the three
noise-trained agents of Table~\ref{tab:seeds}, whose bound does not see the noise, the training episodes
still lasted $180.0\ns$ on average, and those of the nominal agents $177$--$178\ns$: the exploration
noise of the stochastic policy, with the training noise on top of it, keeps the cost of a training rollout
above a threshold that the deterministic plan of the same policy reaches at about $100\ns$.

\emph{The policy learns to respond to noisy states, and that response is not deployed.} During
training the agent observes the noisy propagator and acts on it. We ran every checkpoint of both histories
in the noisy environment at $1\MHz$, in closed loop and for the duration of its own plan, and compared it
with the open-loop replay of that plan (Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism}(b)). In closed loop the noise-optimized
agent \emph{is} the more robust one: it beats the nominal agent in $81\%$ of iterations, by
$5.4\times10^{-3}$ in $\Fbar$ on average ($95\%$ interval $[3.4,7.4]\times10^{-3}$). The reason is that the
nominal agent, fed states it never saw in training, reacts badly: its closed-loop fidelity falls below its
own open-loop value in $99\%$ of iterations, by $8.8\times10^{-3}$ on average, with three times the
variance. Stochastic training removes this instability --- the noise-optimized agent's closed-loop and
open-loop fidelities agree within $10^{-3}$ --- but it neither produces feedback that beats open loop nor
changes the open-loop pulse. A robustness test carried out in the training environment would credit the
noise-optimized agent with an advantage that its deployable pulses do not have.

\label{sec:memory}

The first is that the pulse shape should matter as soon as the noise is correlated in time; the second,
that a noise-aware optimizer should then be able to use it, and should gain nothing under white noise.

$1\MHz$ for the three horizons), as Eq.~\eqref{eq:whitenoise} requires, and the only thing that white-noise training changes is the nominal
fidelity, for the worse: none of the $24$ white-trained pulses is more accurate, or more robust against
the very noise it was trained on, than the pulse optimized without noise at the same horizon. Its
stochastic gradient costs $5$--$10\times10^{-3}$ in nominal fidelity and buys nothing. The null result of
Sec.~\ref{sec:matched} is therefore not a peculiarity of the two TRPO runs: it recurs, with seeds, in an
optimizer whose gradients are exact.

Part of that cost is specific to our objective, in which the leakage bound is accumulated from the noisy
Hamiltonian and responds to the noise itself (Sec.~\ref{sec:noisyleak}). We therefore repeated the
experiment with the bound taken from the noise-free controls, so that the noise enters the objective
through the fidelity alone (rows marked $\ast$ in Table~\ref{tab:adammodels}). The cost of white-noise
training in nominal fidelity falls from $4.8$, $5.1$ and $10.1\times10^{-3}$ to $2.0$, $3.5$ and
$8.1\times10^{-3}$ at the three horizons, and the outcome is the same: again none of the $24$ pulses is more
accurate, or more robust against white noise, than the one optimized without noise. Over the two variants
white-noise training helped in none of $48$ runs.

Quasi-static training does what white training cannot. It lowers the quasi-static sensitivity in every
one of the $24$ runs, by $18$--$24\%$ on average and with a spread of a few percent between seeds, by
moving weight of the filter function away from zero frequency while leaving its average untouched
[Fig.~\ref{fig:memory}(a)]; the three pulses coincide for correlation times below a few nanoseconds and
separate beyond [Fig.~\ref{fig:memory}(b)]. The gain is modest, because a $70$--$130\ns$ pulse through a
$10\MHz$ filter has little room for the refocusing structure of dynamically corrected
gates~\cite{KhodjastehViola2009}, and it is paid for in nominal fidelity, so it shows in $\Fbar$ only once
the noise is strong enough: at $0.1\MHz$ a single quasi-static-trained pulse beats the noise-free one, at
the training strength of $0.3\MHz$ $19$ of $24$ do, against $2$ of $24$ white-trained ones.

Robustness that depends on the pulse shape therefore requires noise with memory --- quasi-static offsets,
drift or low-frequency spectra --- which is what robust-control techniques are designed
for~\cite{Koch2022} and what the UFO noise model does not contain.

\section{Runtime across the gate family}
\label{sec:runtime}

\subsection{Structure of the subfamily $\gamma=\pi/2$}
\label{sec:structure}

The runtime study uses $\gamma=\pi/2$, and there the two-qubit structure can be stated exactly. Since
$\exp(i\tfrac{\pi}{2}\sigma^z_1\sigma^z_2)=i\sigma^z_1\sigma^z_2$ and
$\exp[i\tfrac{\pi}{2}(\sigma^x_1\sigma^x_2+\sigma^y_1\sigma^y_2)]=\sigma^z_1\sigma^z_2$, every member is,
up to a global phase, a pure exchange rotation,
\begin{equation}
\Ntgt{\alpha}{\pi/2}=i\exp\!\bigl[i\varepsilon\bigl(\sigma_1^x\sigma_2^x+\sigma_1^y\sigma_2^y\bigr)\bigr],
\qquad \varepsilon=\alpha-\frac{\pi}{2},
\label{eq:exchange}
\end{equation}
which is the decomposition noted in Ref.~\cite{Niu2019} with its local $Z$ rotations absorbed. Four
consequences are used below.

(i) \emph{Local members.} At $\alpha=\pi/2$ the target is the identity, and at $\alpha=0$ and
$\alpha=\pi$ it is the local gate $\sigma^z_1\sigma^z_2$. Because the fidelity~\eqref{eq:fidelity}
ignores global phases, a pulse that does nothing implements the $\alpha=\pi/2$ target exactly.

(ii) \emph{Entangling class and CNOT count.} In the Weyl chamber~\cite{Zhang2003} each member lies at
$(a,a,0)$ with $a=\min(|\varepsilon|,\pi/2-|\varepsilon|)$, so the most entangling members,
$|\varepsilon|=\pi/4$, are iSWAP-equivalent. Because one canonical coordinate vanishes, every non-local
member needs two CNOTs rather than three~\cite{VidalDawson2004,ShendeMarkovBullock2004}. With the gate
times above and three single-qubit layers, the target-specific synthesis time is
\begin{equation}
\Tsyn^{\mathrm{tgt}}(\alpha)=
\begin{cases}
0 & \alpha=\pi/2,\\
20\ns & \alpha\in\{0,\pi\},\\
150\ns & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:tsyntgt}
\end{equation}
On processors whose native entangler is itself a tunable exchange gate, as in the fSim gate set of
gmon-type devices~\cite{Foxen2020}, each member is a single native two-qubit operation, and the
synthesis overhead disappears altogether.

(iii) \emph{Mirror symmetry.} The parity of the first mode, $P_1=(-1)^{\hat n_1}$, maps
$\hat a_1\to-\hat a_1$. Conjugating Eq.~\eqref{eq:hrwa} by $P_1$ therefore sends $g\to-g$ and
$\phi_1\to\phi_1+\pi$ and leaves everything else unchanged, including the leakage structure and the
boundary penalty, while on the qubits it maps $\varepsilon\to-\varepsilon$. The control problems at
$\alpha$ and $\pi-\alpha$ are thus exactly equivalent: a pulse for one, with those two substitutions,
is a pulse for the other with the same cost.

(iv) \emph{Required exchange area.} In the qubit projection~\eqref{eq:hqb}, the coupling alone
generates Eq.~\eqref{eq:exchange} once $|\int g\,dt|=2|\varepsilon|$, with $g$ in angular units. At the
$20\MHz$ bound this takes at most $25\ns$ for any member. Pulse durations are therefore set by the
bandwidth filter, the boundary conditions and the cost trade-off rather than by the amount of entangling
action required: each pole of the filter~\eqref{eq:filter} has a time constant $1/(\pi B)\approx32\ns$,
and a filtered step needs about $52\ns$ to reach half its amplitude. Section~\ref{sec:physicalinterp}
makes this quantitative: the largest exchange area that a bounded control can deliver through the filter
in a given time is a linear program, and it sets the scale of every runtime in this paper.

\subsection{Protocol}

To test hardware efficiency beyond a single target we sweep the subfamily
\begin{equation}
\Utgt(\alpha)=\Ntgt{\alpha}{\pi/2},\qquad \alpha\in[0,\pi],
\label{eq:runtimefamily}
\end{equation}
in $21$ steps of $0.157$. Rather than retraining
from scratch at each point, the best checkpoint obtained at one $\alpha$ initializes training at the next
--- a curriculum that exploits continuity in target space, in the spirit of the transfer strategy of
Ref.~\cite{Niu2019}. Training at each target stops when the cost falls below $0.40$ or after $150$
iterations, and we call a target \emph{converged} when its final deterministic rollout reaches that cost.
The environment is stochastic at $\strain=1\MHz$. The first target, $\alpha=0$, is a cold start trained
from scratch with a hand-tuned sequence of stopping costs.

As a no-transfer control we also sweep the same family with the Adam baseline, optimizing each of $33$
targets ($\alpha=0,0.1,\dots,3.1$ and $\pi$) independently over the horizon grid of
Table~\ref{tab:config}, with the horizon chosen by the noisy training objective as in Sec.~\ref{sec:adam}.
This separates two effects that the curriculum conflates: how the difficulty of the control problem varies
across the family, and what the curriculum's history adds to it.

\subsection{Runtime landscape of the curriculum}

Figure~\ref{fig:runtime} summarizes the curriculum sweep, and Table~\ref{tab:runtime} in
Appendix~\ref{app:curriculum} lists it target by target. Of the $21$ targets,
$14$ converge. One of them is degenerate: at $\alpha=1.570$ the target is the identity to within
$|\varepsilon|=8\times10^{-4}$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:exchange}, so the cost is already below threshold after the
first $2\ns$ step and the phase ends after one iteration with fidelity $0.999999$ --- the value that doing
nothing at all would give. It is not a fast gate, and we exclude it from every aggregate below.

The $13$ non-trivial converged targets take $76$--$148\ns$ (median $132\ns$), at nominal fidelity
$0.982$--$0.992$ and $\Fbar=0.950$--$0.967$ at $\snoise=1\MHz$. The other $7$ do not converge: the cold
start $\alpha=0$, the interval $\alpha\in[0.47,0.79]$ and the edge $\alpha\ge2.83$. Their final rollouts
run to the $180\ns$ ceiling at nominal fidelity $0.92$--$0.95$ ($0.56$ at $\alpha=0$), and the lowest cost
reached anywhere along them, $0.43$--$0.80$ at $142$--$174\ns$, stays above the threshold. They also carry
somewhat larger leakage (Fig.~\ref{fig:runtime}(c)). These are failures to converge within the budget,
not slow gates.

The mirror symmetry of Sec.~\ref{sec:structure} shows that the failures belong to the optimizer rather
than to the targets. The curriculum grid is symmetric about $\pi/2$ to within $0.002$, and in five of its
ten mirror pairs one side converges while the other fails. Reflecting the converged pulses ($g\to-g$,
$\phi_1\to\phi_1+\pi$) reproduces their cost to $10^{-12}$ on the mirrored targets. For instance, the
$118\ns$ pulse for $\alpha=2.355$ implements $\alpha=\pi-2.355$ with fidelity $0.986$, where the
curriculum itself ended at $180\ns$ and $0.949$. Only the two ends of the interval, both close to the local
gate $\sigma^z_1\sigma^z_2$, fail on both sides. The converged runtimes do not follow the target structure
either: their rank correlation with $|\varepsilon|$ is $0.51$ ($p=0.08$), and with the Weyl coordinate $a$
it is $0.01$. The curriculum landscape records the order in which targets were visited and the stopping
rule (Sec.~\ref{sec:runtimecaveat}) more than the targets themselves.

\subsection{Comparison with gate synthesis}

Measured against the generic reference $\Tsyn=215\ns$, every converged non-trivial target is faster, by
$1.45$--$2.83\times$ (median $1.63\times$). This is the comparison of Ref.~\cite{Niu2019}, and it needs two
qualifications. First, the $180\ns$ episode cap lies below $215\ns$, so even the failed rollouts would
formally beat the reference; a speedup is meaningful only for a pulse that implements the gate. Second, the
reference is generic. Against the target-specific time of Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsyntgt}, $150\ns$ for every
non-local member, the converged curriculum pulses are faster by only $1.01$--$1.97\times$ (median
$1.14\times$), and the local ends of the family, a single layer of single-qubit gates in a circuit, are
exactly where the curriculum fails.

The order-of-magnitude reduction reported in Ref.~\cite{Niu2019} refers to $\alpha\in[1.2,1.7]$. By
Eq.~\eqref{eq:exchange} this interval is the neighbourhood $|\varepsilon|\le0.37$ of the identity, where the
required exchange area can be accumulated in about $6\ns$ at the coupling bound. A large ratio to a fixed
three-CNOT reference is to be expected there from any working pulse optimizer; it reflects the reference
more than the controller. In our sweep the only target in that interval with a ratio of order ten is the
identity itself.

None of this removes the physical point. The synthesis benchmark is optimal within a circuit model over a
fixed gate library, whereas the analog controller optimizes in the continuous Hamiltonian-control space.
But the size of the analog advantage depends on the reference, and a fair reference must be
target-specific --- or, on hardware with a native tunable exchange gate~\cite{Foxen2020}, must be that
gate.

\subsection{What the runtime comparison measures}
\label{sec:runtimecaveat}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{adam_family_sweep.pdf}
\caption{No-transfer control: the Adam baseline swept independently over $33$ targets of
$\Ntgt{\alpha}{\pi/2}$. (a) Selected horizon versus $\alpha$. (b) Nominal fidelity and cost.
(c) Average fidelity at $\snoise=1\MHz$ and the corresponding variance. The horizon is shortest around the
identity target $\alpha=\pi/2$ and grows with the exchange angle $|\alpha-\pi/2|$.}
\label{fig:adamfamily}
\end{figure}

The independently optimized Adam baseline is faster than the curriculum controller across the family. Its
selected horizons span $40$--$80\ns$ with a median of $70\ns$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:adamfamily}), against a median
of $132\ns$ for the converged curriculum targets, at comparable nominal fidelity (median $0.987$ against
$0.988$) and better robustness at $\snoise=1\MHz$ (median $\Fbar=0.969$ against $0.955$) --- the latter as
Eq.~\eqref{eq:whitenoise} predicts for shorter pulses. Against Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsyntgt} the $31$ non-local
Adam solutions are faster by $1.9$--$3.75\times$ (median $2.1\times$), with the same range and median for
the $21$ of them whose nominal fidelity exceeds $0.98$; at the two local ends Adam's
$70\ns$ pulses, with fidelity $0.940$, are slower than a $20\ns$ layer of single-qubit gates. Three caveats
apply to the Adam numbers: the horizon grid starts at $40\ns$, so the seven targets that sit at the grid
minimum could be faster still; the horizon is selected by the noisy objective, which favours short pulses
(Sec.~\ref{sec:noisyleak}); and nine of the $33$ solutions have nominal fidelity below $0.97$. The
synthesis times, moreover, are those of ideal gates with no infidelity attached, whereas the pulses set
against them have fidelities of $0.98$--$0.99$.

The runtime advantage over compiled synthesis is therefore a property of \emph{direct analog optimization
on this hardware model}, not a benefit conferred by reinforcement learning.

The curriculum runtimes are, in addition, partly an artifact of the stopping rule. Training at each target
halts as soon as the UFO cost drops below the advancement threshold $0.40$, and every converged phase ends
in the narrow band $C\in[0.393,0.400]$. The agent stops improving the moment it becomes good enough to
advance and has no incentive to shorten the pulse further, so the curriculum runtimes are times sufficient
to reach a fixed cost under a bounded budget, not the shortest times the policy could achieve. A tighter
threshold would very likely move the curve down; we did not run that sweep.

\subsection{Physical interpretation}
\label{sec:physicalinterp}

Equation~\eqref{eq:exchange} gives the family a single physical coordinate, the exchange angle
$|\varepsilon|=|\alpha-\pi/2|$ that the coupling must accumulate. The direct optimizer, which solves each
target independently, follows it closely. Its horizon is $40\ns$ for all seven targets with
$|\varepsilon|\le0.33$, rises to $50$--$60\ns$ for $|\varepsilon|\in[0.37,0.67]$ and to $70$--$80\ns$ beyond,
and drops back to $70\ns$ only at the two ends, where the target approaches a local gate; the rank
correlation is $0.80$ ($p<10^{-7}$), and $0.97$ for $|\varepsilon|\le1.3$. It does not follow the
entangling content: the iSWAP-equivalent members, the most entangling of the family, sit at $70\ns$ like
the local ends, and the rank correlation with the Weyl coordinate $a$ is $0.27$ ($p=0.14$).

The mechanism is the one the Hamiltonian suggests. The native coupling generates every member directly,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:exchange}, so what varies across the family is not how well the target aligns with the
hardware --- every member aligns perfectly --- but how much exchange area the pulse must accumulate
through a bandwidth-limited channel that must also start and end at zero. Near $\alpha=\pi/2$ the target is
close to the identity and the pulse can be short and gentle; toward the ends it must be longer, and at the
ends a local-gate alternative appears that the optimizer exploits only partly. The favourable central
region of both sweeps is therefore the neighbourhood of the identity, not a basin of especially
hardware-friendly entanglers.

This can be made quantitative. The filter~\eqref{eq:filter} is linear, so the largest exchange area
$\int g\,dt$ that a control bounded by $20\MHz$ can deliver in $N$ steps is a linear program, with or
without the condition that the filtered coupling is back at zero at the last step, which the boundary term
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ufo} enforces softly. Requiring the area $2|\varepsilon|$ of Sec.~\ref{sec:structure}
gives the band of Fig.~\ref{fig:runtime}(a): from $28$--$40\ns$ at $|\varepsilon|=0.16$ to $74$--$96\ns$
at the ends of the family. It is an estimate for pulses that use the coupling alone, not a speed limit of
the model; near the ends the optimizer also uses the detunings. The horizons selected by the direct
optimizer lie inside the band for $20$ of the $26$ targets that are not pinned at the $40\ns$ grid
minimum. The main exceptions are the five targets with $|\varepsilon|\ge1.43$, where the selected $70\ns$
is shorter than even the lower edge: there the pulses are detuning pulses whose area falls short of the
$\pi$ that the local gate needs, which is why their fidelity stops at $0.94$--$0.97$. The converged
curriculum pulses are $1.3$--$3.3$ times longer than the upper edge (median $1.8$). Within this model,
then, the durations chosen by the direct optimizer largely track the coupling-only bound, and those of the
learned pulses do not.

The same linear program applies to the single-qubit gates of the synthesis reference. A rotation by
$\pi$ needs a drive area $\pi/2$, which the filtered, bounded drive delivers in no less than $54\ns$, or
$72\ns$ if it must end at zero, and a $\pi/2$ rotation needs $40$ and $56\ns$, against the $20\ns$ per
layer assumed in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:tsyn} and~\eqref{eq:tsyntgt}. Both synthesis references therefore
belong to hardware with a wider control bandwidth than the model in which the pulses are optimized, and
the ratio between the two measures that mismatch as much as it measures analog control.

Table~\ref{tab:claims} sets the claims of Ref.~\cite{Niu2019} against our findings. The framework itself
reproduces. Rebuilt from the published description, it produces genuine analog two-qubit gates in pulses
shorter than the compiled reference; the original UFO weights sit close to the best region of an
independent weight sweep, and the original network layout is as good as any we tried within the
seed-to-seed spread (Appendix~\ref{app:modelsel}). The curriculum strategy works in the sense
Ref.~\cite{Niu2019} describes: once warm-started, seven of the thirteen non-trivial converged targets
were solved within $20$ iterations.

The absolute fidelities are lower than those reported. Ref.~\cite{Niu2019} quotes gate infidelities of
order $10^{-3}$ for every member of the family (its Fig.~3); our $13$ non-trivial converged targets have
nominal infidelities of $0.8$--$1.8\times10^{-2}$, and $7$ of the $21$ targets do not converge
(Sec.~\ref{sec:runtime}). On $\Ntgt{2.2}{\pi/2}$ the gap is smaller: at the lowest noise strength the
average fidelity is $0.995$ there, against $0.9950$ and $0.9925$ for our nominal and noise-optimized
agents (Table~\ref{tab:robust}). We have not identified the cause; the training budget, the single seed
and the coarser time step are candidates.

Two rows of the table need a comment.

\emph{The gradient baseline.} The SGD baseline of Ref.~\cite{Niu2019} was also Adam with a noisy
objective, and it received about $2000$ random restarts, yet its average fidelity on this same target
stays near $0.75$--$0.77$ over the whole noise range of its Fig.~4. We cannot reconstruct why; the gap
between the two gradient baselines is larger than any gap between RL and gradient optimization that we
observe. Our own comparison is the least general of our findings. It sets one gradient optimizer against
our TRPO agents, which reach cost $0.30$--$0.35$ where the optimizer reaches $0.16$ and whose curriculum
fails on a third of the family; a better-trained agent could narrow the gap.

\emph{The size of the reported robustness gain.} An order-of-magnitude difference in infidelity between
two open-loop pulses of similar fidelity and length cannot arise under this noise model. Differences of
that size can only come from different nominal fidelities or durations, or from an evaluation in which the
policy acts on the noisy state (Sec.~\ref{sec:noisyleak}); Ref.~\cite{Niu2019} does not give enough detail
to tell which, if either, applies to its results.

\subsection{Comparing noise scales}
\label{sec:noisescales}

One further point limits any quantitative comparison with Ref.~\cite{Niu2019}. Because the kicks are
independent from step to step, the noise power per unit bandwidth is $\snoise^2\Delta t$, and $K$ in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:whitenoise} is proportional to $\Delta t$: the same $\snoise$ describes a different physical
noise level at a different time step, and it matters whether amplitudes are quoted in cyclic or angular
units. Replaying our pulses with the noise redrawn every $0.1$, $0.5$, $1$, $4$ or $8\ns$ instead of
every $2\ns$, at the same $\snoise$, multiplies the excess infidelity by $0.06$, $0.31$, $0.6$, $1.3$ and
$2.5$, as the first-order rate predicts to within $5\%$. It also matters where the noise enters. Added to the raw controls,
before the bandwidth filter instead of after it, the same $\snoise$ costs our five pulses $5$ to $18$ times
less infidelity, and reaches the Hamiltonian with the $32\ns$ memory of the filter. In physical terms, the
detuning noise of our model at $\snoise=1\MHz$ and $\Delta t=2\ns$ is white frequency noise that dephases
each qubit in $T_\varphi=2/[(2\pi\snoise)^2\Delta t]\approx25\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, and $3.5\MHz$ corresponds to
$2\,\mu\mathrm{s}$.

Ref.~\cite{Niu2019} describes its pulses as piecewise constant with ``around one thousand time steps''
each, which for gates of about $100\ns$ is a step of about $0.1\ns$, twenty times shorter than ours; it
does not state its convention for $\snoise$. At that step the same $\snoise$ costs sixteen times less
infidelity, and the fidelity loss that its Fig.~4 shows over $\snoise\in[0.1,3.5]\MHz$ is indeed more
than an order of magnitude smaller than ours for pulses of comparable length: there the average fidelity
of the noise-optimized controller stays between $0.995$ and $0.98$, while ours falls from $0.9925$ to
$0.713$ (Table~\ref{tab:robust}). Dividing our loss by sixteen gives about $0.991$ at $1\MHz$, so the time
step alone accounts for a difference of that size; angular units or noise injected before the filter would
change it further. The $\snoise$ axes of the two studies should not be identified, and no number in this
paper at a given $\snoise$ should be compared with a number at the same $\snoise$ in Ref.~\cite{Niu2019}.
The conclusions above do not depend on the scale: Eq.~\eqref{eq:whitenoise} holds at every noise strength,
and the matched comparison is negative over the whole range.

The same applies to the training noise. Under the same assumptions, $\strain=1\MHz$ at $2\ns$ corresponds
to about $4\MHz$ at $0.1\ns$, above the range evaluated in Ref.~\cite{Niu2019}. We did not train at the
matched weaker level; by Eq.~\eqref{eq:whitenoise} the pulse-independence does not depend on it, but the
quality of the trained agent may.

The step matters in a second way. The part of the leakage bound that responds to white noise
(Sec.~\ref{sec:noisyleak}) contains a fourth finite difference of the controls and scales as
$\Delta t^{-4}$: replayed on finer grids, its growth of $3.8\times10^{-3}$ per ns at $2\ns$ becomes about $15$,
$250$ and $4200$ times larger at $1$, $0.5$ and $0.25\ns$. On a grid of a thousand steps a bound
accumulated from the noisy Hamiltonian would exceed every other term of the objective by orders of
magnitude, so Ref.~\cite{Niu2019} presumably evaluates it otherwise; it does not say how. The domination
of the noisy objective by the bound (Sec.~\ref{sec:noisyleak}) is therefore a property of our implementation, and we cannot tell
whether it applies to theirs. The direct-optimizer test of Sec.~\ref{sec:memory} and the matched
comparison of TRPO agents (Table~\ref{tab:seeds}) were both repeated without it, and their outcome did not
change.

\subsection{Why the discrepancies arise}

Some differences are what independent reimplementation means. The two studies differ in discretization,
filtering pipeline, stopping rules, curriculum schedule, training budget and evaluation protocol, the
optimization problem is high-dimensional and non-convex, and a single run of any configuration carries a
seed-to-seed spread of several $10^{-3}$ in $\Fbar$ at $1\MHz$ (Appendix~\ref{app:modelsel}). Exact
numerical coincidence is not the appropriate standard.

The main discrepancy is not an implementation detail. Two properties of the UFO formulation under white
control noise follow from the formulation itself, and we would expect them in any faithful implementation:
the pulse-independence of Eq.~\eqref{eq:whitenoise}, and the fact that a policy observing the noisy
propagator learns to respond to it rather than to produce a robust open-loop pulse. They are the reason we
read the robustness results of Ref.~\cite{Niu2019} as not established, rather than as not reproduced for
incidental reasons. The domination of the noisy objective by the leakage bound, by contrast, depends on
where the bound is evaluated and on the time step (Sec.~\ref{sec:noisescales}) and may not carry over; the
conclusion on white-noise training does not rest on it (Secs.~\ref{sec:matched} and~\ref{sec:memory}).

\subsection{Relation to hardware}
\label{sec:hardware}

This paper compares optimizers on a common model, and none of its fidelities is a statement about a
device. Five remarks place that model against current hardware.

\emph{Noise.} Frequency and amplitude noise in superconducting circuits is dominated by low-frequency
components --- $1/f$ flux and charge noise and slow drift of the calibration~\cite{Krantz2019,Paladino2014}.
Of the noise models of Sec.~\ref{sec:robust} the quasi-static one is therefore the closer to a device, and
the white model of Ref.~\cite{Niu2019}, under which no training can help, the further.

\emph{Decoherence.} The model is closed. Weak relaxation and pure dephasing, at rates $\Gamma_1=1/T_1$ and
$\Gamma_\varphi=1/T_\varphi$ on each qubit, lower the average fidelity of any two-qubit gate of duration
$T$ by $\tfrac{4}{5}T(\Gamma_1+\Gamma_\varphi)$, whatever the gate~\cite{Abad2022}: like white control
noise, Markovian decoherence leaves a pulse nothing to exploit but its duration. For
$T_1=T_\varphi=100\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, of the order of current processors~\cite{GoogleQAI2025}, this is
$1.1\times10^{-3}$ for the $70\ns$ Adam pulse and $1.9\times10^{-3}$ for the $116\ns$ TRPO pulse,
comparable to the nominal infidelities of Table~\ref{tab:single}. By the same formula the white noise of
the model at $1\MHz$ costs as much as $T_1=T_\varphi\approx5\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, and the runtime weight of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ufo} corresponds to $T_1=T_\varphi\approx12\,\mu\mathrm{s}$. Both describe devices an order
of magnitude less coherent than today's; a weight matched to current coherence would move the optimum of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ufo} towards longer and more accurate pulses.

\emph{Bandwidth.} The $10\MHz$ filter is narrow by hardware standards. Sycamore processors, descendants of
the gmon, drive single-qubit gates with $25\ns$ pulses and perform iSWAP-like gates by turning on a
$20\MHz$ coupling for $12\ns$~\cite{Arute2019}: the amplitude bound of the present model, four to six
times faster than the $54$--$72\ns$ that its filter allows for the same exchange angle
(Sec.~\ref{sec:physicalinterp}). The runtimes of Sec.~\ref{sec:runtime} are thus set by a modelling choice
of Ref.~\cite{Niu2019} more than by the coupler.

\emph{Higher levels.} Those fast gates are not members of the benchmark family. The coupling shifts
$\ket{11}$ through $\ket{02}$ and $\ket{20}$ by $4g^2/|\eta|$, so an exchange pulse carries a conditional
phase $\phi=(4/|\eta|)\int g^2dt$, which $\Ntgt{\alpha}{\pi/2}$ does not contain and local rotations
cannot remove; an otherwise perfect pulse is left with $1-F=\sin^2(\phi/4)$. This is what limits the
pulses optimized without noise in Sec.~\ref{sec:memory}, whose nominal infidelities equal
$\sin^2(\phi/4)$ to within $1\%$. In this model the fidelity of an exchange pulse therefore improves only
as $1/T^2$, a trade-off that native gate sets avoid by making $\phi$ part of the gate~\cite{Foxen2020} or
by cancelling it through the level structure of the coupler~\cite{Sung2021}.

\emph{Absolute fidelity.} Two-qubit gates on tunable-coupler transmons reach a Pauli error of
$3.8\times10^{-3}$ for a continuous fSim family on gmon qubits~\cite{Foxen2020}, and fidelities of
$99.76\%$ and $99.87\%$ for CZ and iSWAP gates~\cite{Sung2021}, decoherence included. The closed-system
fidelities of Table~\ref{tab:single} are not competitive pulse designs and are not offered as such: what
transfers from this study is the comparison between methods and the two structural results of
Sec.~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

\subsection{Limitations}

The study is simulation-based. The model is truncated to three levels per mode under a rotating-wave
description, and it has no transfer-function nonlinearities, spurious modes, drift or open-system
decoherence. The noise used in the reproduction and in the RL training is Gaussian and white, and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:whitenoise} is specific to it. We did not train an RL policy under noise with memory.
Whether such a policy finds the pulse shapes that the gradient optimizer finds under quasi-static noise
(Sec.~\ref{sec:memory}), or better ones, is the most important open question this work leaves.

The agents of Table~\ref{tab:single}, of Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}, of the closed-loop test and of the runtime
sweep were each trained with a single seed, in stages with changing stopping costs
(Table~\ref{tab:config}); only the matched comparison was repeated with three seeds
(Table~\ref{tab:seeds}). Conclusions that rest on large effects are established: the absence of an
order-of-magnitude robustness gain, the direct optimizer's lead of about $0.012$ in $\Fbar$ at $1\MHz$,
and the runtime results. Differences of a few $10^{-3}$ between two agents are not. All agents were
trained at a time step of $2\ns$, about twenty times coarser than that of Ref.~\cite{Niu2019}, because
each TRPO agent already takes $9.5\times10^7$ simulator steps; the consequences for the noise scale and
for the leakage bound are discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:noisescales}. Their training episodes almost never
stopped early (Sec.~\ref{sec:noisyleak}), and an implementation in which the stopping time is learned
under noise could behave differently, although Eq.~\eqref{eq:whitenoise} would still bound what its pulses
can gain.

The direct optimizer is deliberately minimal --- one initialization and $400$ iterations per horizon on a
$10\ns$ grid starting at $40\ns$ --- and is not a survey of gradient methods such as
automatic-differentiation GRAPE or Krotov. The comparison is between one RL framework and one gradient
optimizer on one hardware model, not a ranking of control methodologies, and it does not include
model-based RL~\cite{Khalid2023}. That gradients do well when the model is known is not new; what this
paper adds is the mechanism of Eq.~\eqref{eq:whitenoise} and the structure of the benchmark family.

\subsection{Relation to subsequent work}

Read from the present vantage point, the lasting contribution of Ref.~\cite{Niu2019} looks less like a
particular optimizer and more like a demonstration that robust analog control can be posed as a learning
problem with leakage-aware costs and stochastic perturbations. The literature since has refined that in two
directions. Model-free and hardware-interacting schemes reduce model bias by adapting on the device
itself~\cite{Sivak2022}, and model-based or hybrid methods address the sample inefficiency that makes pure
model-free RL expensive~\cite{Khalid2023}. Our results sharpen the case for both. When the model is known
and the noise is white, a direct optimizer wins both the nominal and the robustness problem, simply by
producing short and accurate pulses. What a noise-trained policy does learn is feedback, which is useful
only where the state can be observed during the operation. Learning therefore earns its cost where the
model is uncertain or the noise has structure that a pulse can exploit --- the regime of closed-loop
refinement against the device, where the perturbation distribution is the device's own rather than a
modelled one~\cite{Vetter2024,Glaser2025,Wang2024}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have independently reimplemented the UFO framework for robust analog two-qubit gate design on a
multilevel gmon model and compared it with a direct gradient optimizer that uses the same Hamiltonian, the
same objective and the same training noise. The framework works: it produces high-fidelity analog gates,
faster than a compiled implementation, and its published hyperparameters are close to optimal in our
hands. Three of its headline claims are not supported in our implementation. The learned controller does
not outperform a direct optimizer, training under noise does not make the deployed pulses more robust, and
the order-of-magnitude runtime gain sits around a target that is the identity.

Two results explain these findings and should transfer beyond this study. Against temporally uncorrelated
control noise, the open-loop robustness of any pulse is fixed to leading order by its nominal fidelity and
its duration, Eq.~\eqref{eq:whitenoise}, with a rate that we compute from the Hamiltonian and verify on
every pulse we produced; no training procedure can improve on that except by making pulses more accurate or
shorter. And every member of the benchmark subfamily $\Ntgt{\alpha}{\pi/2}$ is an exchange rotation by
$\alpha-\pi/2$, which makes its runtime landscape a function of the exchange angle and fixes the fair
synthesis reference at two CNOTs.

The useful question for noise-aware learning in quantum control is therefore not how large its advantage is
under white noise --- there, to leading order, there is none to be had --- but whether it finds pulse shapes
that exploit noise with memory, as a gradient optimizer trained against quasi-static noise already does
to a modest degree, and whether the robustness it reports is that of a deployable open-loop
pulse or of a feedback policy. Benchmarks that separate those two cases, and that state the time step and
units of their noise model, would make results in this area comparable.

\section{The TSWT coherent leakage bound}
\label{app:tswt}

We summarize the derivation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:leakbound}. Starting from the decomposition
Eq.~\eqref{eq:decomp} in the regime $\epsilon/\Delta\ll1$, one seeks an anti-Hermitian generator
$\hat S(t)=\epsilon\hat S_1+\epsilon^2\hat S_2+\dots$ such that the transformed Hamiltonian
Eq.~\eqref{eq:tswt} is block-diagonal to the desired order. Order by order, the block-off-diagonal
components must satisfy
\begin{equation}
[\hat H_0,\hat S_1]=-\hat H_2,\qquad
[\hat H_0,\hat S_2]=-\bigl([\hat H_1,\hat S_1]-i\dot{\hat S}_1\bigr)_{\mathrm{od}},
\label{eq:generators}
\end{equation}
which are solved elementwise on the off-diagonal blocks by dividing by the subspace energy differences
$E_\alpha-E_{\alpha'}$; the assumption $\epsilon/\Delta\ll1$ is what makes these divisions safe. After
second order the residual block-off-diagonal Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}
H_{\mathrm{od}}^{(3)}=[\hat H_1,\hat S_2]+\tfrac{1}{3}\bigl[[\hat H_2,\hat S_1],\hat S_1\bigr]-i\dot{\hat S}_2
=O\!\left(\frac{\epsilon^3}{\Delta^2}\right).
\label{eq:hod3}
\end{equation}
The leakage amplitude out of $\Omega_0$ over $[0,T]$ is then an interaction-picture integral of
$\Hod$ against a rapidly oscillating phase $e^{i\int\Delta dt}$. Repeated integration by parts
converts this into boundary terms, which pick up $\|\Hod\|/\Delta$ at $t=0$ and $t=T$, plus a residual
integral controlled by the second derivative of $\Hod$ divided by $\Delta^2$. Collecting these gives
Eq.~\eqref{eq:leakbound}.

The two contributions dominate in complementary regimes: for slow, off-resonant modulation the
boundary terms dominate, and for faster on-resonant modulation the derivative term does. This is why
the bound is usable as a single penalty across the operating range rather than only in one limit.

\section{Algorithmic details}
\label{app:algo}

\paragraph*{Environment step.}
Given the current observation and propagator: map the action to physical controls; apply the bandwidth
filter Eq.~\eqref{eq:filter}; if stochastic training is enabled, sample Gaussian perturbations for
$g,\delta_{1,2},f_{1,2}$ and $\eta$ and wrap the phases modulo $2\pi$; build $H_t$ from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:hrwa}; propagate $U_{t+1}=e^{-iH_t\Delta t}U_t$ by exact diagonalization; update the
leakage estimator with the same, possibly noisy, $H_t$; evaluate the running cost Eq.~\eqref{eq:ufo} and
reward $r=-C$; test the stopping
condition (time limit or cost threshold); emit the next observation Eq.~\eqref{eq:obs}.

\paragraph*{Incremental leakage estimator.}
At each step: decompose $H_t$ into $H_0,H_1,H_2$; solve Eq.~\eqref{eq:generators} for $S_1$; estimate
$\dot S_1$ from the stored previous value by finite differences; solve for $S_2$; estimate $\dot S_2$;
form $H_{\mathrm{od}}^{(3)}$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:hod3} and symmetrize; take its spectral norm. The first
and last samples update the boundary contribution; the discrete second derivative, formed from the two
previous stored values, updates the integral contribution. Storing only the two previous states keeps
the estimator $O(1)$ per step.

\paragraph*{TRPO loop.}
Per iteration: collect a batch of stochastic rollouts in parallel workers; compute discounted returns
and generalized advantage estimates~\cite{Schulman2016GAE}; take the KL-constrained policy step
Eq.~\eqref{eq:trpo} by conjugate gradient with backtracking line search; refit the value network by
regression on the empirical returns; evaluate the deterministic policy in the nominal environment and
store the control plan if it improves on the best so far. Checkpoints and induced control plans are
saved every iteration, so that comparisons can be made at the level of the realized pulse rather than
only at the level of network weights.

\paragraph*{Adam baseline.}
For each candidate horizon the raw parameters are initialized to zero, which after the $\tanh$ map gives
zero amplitudes and phases $\pi$. Each of the $400$ steps filters the controls, simulates one trajectory
perturbed at $\strain$, evaluates the UFO cost including the leakage bound on that noisy trajectory,
back-propagates, clips the gradient norm at $10$ and takes an Adam step. Section~\ref{sec:controllers}
describes how the stored plan and the horizon are selected.

\section{First-order response to control noise}
\label{app:firstorder}

Let the noise on channel $c$ take the value $\xi_{c,k}$ (in MHz) during step $k$, so that it adds
$s\,\xi_{c,k}\hat G_c$ to the Hamiltonian $\hat H_k$ of that step, with $s=2\pi\times10^{-3}$ and
$\hat G_c$ the operator that the channel multiplies in Eq.~\eqref{eq:hrwa}. To first order the noisy
propagator is $U=U_0\bigl[1-is\sum_{c,k}\xi_{c,k}\tilde G_{c,k}\bigr]$, with the toggling-frame generators
\begin{equation}
\tilde G_{c,k}=W_k^\dagger\Bigl[\int_0^{\Delta t}e^{i\hat H_k\tau}\hat G_c\,e^{-i\hat H_k\tau}d\tau\Bigr]W_k ,
\label{eq:generators-noise}
\end{equation}
where $U_0$ is the nominal propagator and $W_k$ its part up to the start of step $k$. For zero-mean
Gaussian noise with covariance $\langle\xi_{c,k}\xi_{c',l}\rangle=\delta_{cc'}C(k,l)$, and a nominal gate
that reaches its target, the average fidelity~\eqref{eq:favg} is, to leading order,
\begin{align}
1-\Fbar&\simeq1-\Fbar(0)+\frac{d}{d+1}\,s^2\sum_c\sum_{k,l}C(k,l)\,v_c(k,l),
\nonumber\\
v_c(k,l)&=\frac{\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(P\tilde G_{c,k}\tilde G_{c,l}P\bigr)}{d}
\nonumber\\
&\quad-\frac{\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(P\tilde G_{c,k}P\bigr)\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(P\tilde G_{c,l}P\bigr)}{d^2}.
\label{eq:firstorder}
\end{align}
For stationary noise the double sum is the overlap of the noise spectrum with the filter function
$\Phi_c(f)=\sum_{k,l}v_c(k,l)\cos[2\pi f(t_k-t_l)]$ of the
pulse~\cite{Green2012,Green2013,Cerfontaine2021}. White noise, $C(k,l)=\snoise^2\delta_{kl}$, keeps only
the diagonal $v_c(k,k)$, which is the variance $V$ of Sec.~\ref{sec:whitelimit} evaluated on
$\tilde G_{c,k}$ and does not depend on $W_k$ as long as the nominal evolution keeps the computational
subspace invariant: this is Eq.~\eqref{eq:whitenoise}, and in the frequency domain it is the statement
that the average of $\Phi_c$ over the Nyquist band is the same for every pulse of a given duration.
Quasi-static noise, $C(k,l)=\snoise^2$, keeps the whole sum, $\Phi_c(0)$, in which generators at different
times can add or cancel; that is where the pulse shape enters.

We evaluate Eq.~\eqref{eq:generators-noise} exactly, in the eigenbasis of each $\hat H_k$, and check
Eq.~\eqref{eq:firstorder} against Monte Carlo replays of the full dynamics with noise of the same
covariance. Table~\ref{tab:channels} gives the white-noise case channel by channel.

\subsection{Actor--critic architecture}

$\snoise=1\MHz$ simultaneously.

Two conclusions follow. First, architecture is not neutral: moderate changes in width and depth
produce measurable differences in the final trade-off. Second, the dependence is not monotonic. The
deepest model tested achieves the \emph{lowest} mean leakage of the sweep and simultaneously the
\emph{worst} aggregate cost, the weakest robustness and the lowest nominal fidelity
(Fig.~\ref{fig:archpareto}). Minimizing one term of a multi-objective cost is not a selection
principle.

The best aggregate performer is $(64,64,64)$, but its margin over the paper layout $(64,32,32)$ is
$0.2\%$ in evaluation cost, well inside the seed-to-seed spread: the three seeds of $(64,32,32)$ alone
reach $\Fbar_{\sigma=1}=0.959$, $0.962$ and $0.965$. We therefore retain the paper architecture
$(64,32,32)$ for all production runs, which maximizes comparability with Ref.~\cite{Niu2019}. The same
spread sets the resolution of every single-seed comparison in the paper: differences of a few $10^{-3}$ in
$\Fbar$ at $1\MHz$ between two individual runs are not significant on their own.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the UFO weights}
\label{sec:weights}

The weights in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ufo} are part of the physical definition of the problem, not optimizer
settings, so they deserve separate treatment. We swept
$\chi\in\{5,10,20\}$, $\mu\in\{0.1,0.2,0.4\}$, $\kappa\in\{0.05,0.1,0.2,0.4\}$ at fixed $\beta=10$
($36$ configurations, two seeds each, five iterations of $10\,000$ episodes with stopping cost $0.15$).
Holding $\beta$ fixed makes the sweep a study of the ratios
$\chi/\beta$, $\mu/\beta$, $\kappa/\beta$, which is the physically meaningful reading and keeps the
design tractable.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{ufo_weight_sweep_robustness.pdf}
\caption{Sensitivity of the average fidelity at $\snoise=1\MHz$ to the UFO weights, shown separately
for the fidelity weight $\chi$, the boundary weight $\mu$ and the runtime weight $\kappa$. Underweighting
fidelity ($\chi=5$) opens a failure mode near $\Fbar\approx0.82$; $\mu=0.4$ is strongly bimodal;
$\kappa$ has almost no effect in this fixed-horizon regime.}
\label{fig:ufoweights}
\end{figure}

A methodological caveat governs the whole sweep: the best evaluation cost is \emph{not} a valid
selection criterion across configurations, because different weights define different scalarizations
and their numerical costs are not commensurable. Since all runs here terminate at the same evaluation
time of $180\ns$, increasing $\kappa$ mostly adds a rigid offset. We therefore compare on physical
quantities only --- nominal fidelity, leakage, and robustness at $\snoise=1\MHz$.

The fidelity weight has the clearest effect (Fig.~\ref{fig:ufoweights}). At $\chi=5$ the distribution
is broad and several runs collapse to $\Fbar\approx0.81$--$0.83$; at $\chi=10$ and $\chi=20$ it tightens
around $0.92$--$0.95$. Underweighting logical accuracy relative to leakage lets the optimizer settle
for controls that are clean but simply not the right gate. The boundary weight behaves
non-monotonically: $\mu=0.2$ gives the best median trade-off, $\mu=0.1$ leaves slightly more leakage,
and $\mu=0.4$ becomes strongly bimodal, producing both some of the best runs of the sweep and its worst
lower-tail failures. Larger $\mu$ does reduce the leakage proxy on average --- the lowest-leakage region
is often at $\mu=0.4$ --- but this does not translate into better robust control, which is the same
lesson the architecture sweep taught. The runtime weight $\kappa$ is nearly inert here, for the
structural reason noted above; its influence reappears in Sec.~\ref{sec:runtime}, where gate duration
is the observable rather than a fixed boundary condition.

The Pareto-favourable region is occupied jointly by $(\chi,\mu)\approx(10,0.2)$ and
$(\chi,\mu)\approx(20,0.4)$, with the latter marginally ahead. Given two seeds and a small budget, that
margin does not justify departing from the reference scalarization, so we retain the weights of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:weights} throughout.
